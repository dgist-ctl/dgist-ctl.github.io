🤖 The Rise of Agentic AI · Agentic AI
1

Agentic AI Completes Entire Courses — A Question That Challenges the Purpose of Higher Education

In February 2026, Inside Higher Ed reported that "AI agents can now complete entire coursework autonomously." Beyond simple ghostwriting, an era where autonomous AI achieves all learning objectives has arrived.

  • AI agents: autonomously set goals → gather information → complete tasks → submit work
  • Northeastern University + Anthropic partnership: officially integrating AI agents for student advising and support
  • Core question: "What is the point of teaching what AI can already do?"
  • Canvas IgniteAI Agent (Mar 2026): An AI teaching agent embedded in the LMS used by 40% of North American higher education institutions — automates low-value instructor tasks like rubric generation and student engagement pattern analysis; now used by 30,000+ faculty
  • Vygotsky — Scaffolding vs. Substitution: When an AI agent completes an entire task without assessing a student's Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), it constitutes substitution rather than support — if external assistance does not lead to internalization, independent competence cannot form; the goal of ZPD is to gradually withdraw support so the learner becomes self-sufficient
  • Dweck — Fixed Mindset reinforcement pathway: When AI repeatedly completes difficult tasks on behalf of students, it reinforces the belief that "hard things should be done by AI" — this structurally blocks Productive Struggle, the core experience through which Growth Mindset is formed (the belief that effort changes ability)

[Media]Source: Inside Higher Ed, Feb 2026 · UPCEA Agentic University 2026–27 · Canvas IgniteAI, Mar 2026

2

Learning Science Becomes the Empirical Standard for AI Instructional Design — What Separates "Effective AI" from the Rest

The most critical shift in AI pedagogy research in 2025–2026: the question is no longer which AI tool to use, but which cognitive mechanisms it activates by design. Core principles from Learning Science are now the benchmark for evaluating AI instructional design.

  • Retrieval Practice: When AI prompts students to recall rather than simply providing answers, learning effectiveness triples (Roediger cumulative studies)
  • Spaced Repetition: AI tutors that automatically adjust review timing based on the forgetting curve achieve 2× long-term retention compared to simple repetition
  • Elaborative Interrogation: AI designs that repeatedly ask "Why is that the case?" significantly deepen conceptual understanding
  • Hattie Visible Learning benchmark: Research is converging on the finding that high-impact strategies — feedback (d=0.70), metacognitive strategies (d=0.60), distributed practice (d=0.65) — maintain their effectiveness when implemented in AI design; conversely, AI adoption without learning science principles may fall below the baseline effect size (d=0.40) (Visible Learning: The Sequel, Routledge, 2023)
  • Hattie's key distinction: teachers must be "Activators" rather than "Facilitators" — AI tools that simply deliver information risk reducing teachers to facilitators; whether teachers maintain their role as activators who directly design and orchestrate learning strategies is the key determinant of AI adoption effectiveness
  • Dylan Wiliam — Feedback that moves learning forward: Whether AI feedback is "confirming a correct answer" or "forward-moving feedback that reveals where and why a student is stuck" is a critical distinction — deploying elaborative questioning alone without designing for forward-moving feedback causes question-fatigue backlash; Wiliam warns that feedback making learners dependent on feedback impedes learning

[Research]Source: Hattie — Visible Learning + AI · Retrieval Practice Research

MIT Cognitive Debt — AI Use Reduces Brain Connectivity by 55%

An EEG brainwave study published on arXiv by MIT Media Lab in June 2025 has been widely cited in the education community since its release. The group that used ChatGPT showed neural network connectivity up to 55% lower than the group that wrote independently.

※ Published on arXiv and indexed on PMC/NIH (2025); peer review submission complete, under review — caution advised when interpreting results.
  • Study design: 54 participants wearing EEG devices, comparing AI group / search group / direct writing group
  • AI group: produced better writing — but could not quote their own text and showed the lowest sense of ownership
  • Notable finding: Brain activity did not recover when students who started with AI then switched to writing independently
  • Research conclusion: "Introducing AI after sufficient self-directed cognitive effort maximizes learning outcomes"
  • Vygotsky — Internalization failure: The phenomenon where brain activity fails to recover after AI use can be interpreted as external support failing to convert into internal competence — in ZPD theory, the ultimate goal of scaffolding is for learners to perform independently through progressive "fading"; the MIT study suggests AI intervention may block this internalization pathway itself
  • Dweck — Cognitive debt and mindset: When accumulated experiences of avoiding cognitive effort through AI dependence pile up, students attribute their struggles with difficult tasks to "I was just never good at this," reinforcing a Fixed Mindset — Growth Mindset develops through failure and struggle, and when AI removes the struggle experience, mindset development itself is inhibited (note: preprint-stage study, interpret with caution)

[Preprint]Source: MIT Media Lab — Your Brain on ChatGPT · PMC/NIH entry · arXiv 2506.08872

Ohio State: AI Fluency Mandatory for All Students — Not "Prohibited" but "Required"

In 2025, Ohio State University made AI Fluency coursework mandatory for all enrolled students regardless of major or year. It has become a landmark case representing a paradigm shift in U.S. higher education.

  • Core content: How to use AI tools + understanding limitations + responsible use + critical evaluation
  • Anthropic also launched 3 official AI Fluency courses for educators, students, and institutions (2025)
  • U.S. undergraduate AI degree programs: 90 in 2024 → 193 in 2025 (+114%)
  • From "AI literacy" to "AI fluency" — the standard has shifted from basic understanding to actual applied competency

[Media]Source: Fortune, Jun 2025 · Ohio State AI Fluency

The Shift in AI Academic Integrity Policy — "Using AI Is Not the Violation; Hiding It Is"

As of October 2025, analysis of AI policies at the world's top 100 universities shows most have shifted from outright bans to a three-tier regulatory framework. Transparency — not detection technology — has become the standard.

  • Ideation/brainstorming phase: permitted / Editing/polishing phase: conditional / Drafting: restricted
  • Harvard, Oxford, Michigan: mandatory inclusion of "AI use disclosure language" in course syllabi
  • Declining reliance on AI detection tools — false positive problems leading to increased legal disputes
  • 2026 alternative: shifting to process-based assessment using draft history, notes, and revision trails
  • HEPI Apr 2026: The "AI innovation vs. academic integrity" binary framing is itself a false setup — a new framework of transparency-based integration as mutually compatible is gaining traction
  • Dylan Wiliam — Root cause of the integrity crisis: The surge in academic integrity violations is fundamentally a symptom of assessment design failure — if an assignment is designed to reveal whether a student truly understands, AI ghostwriting becomes pointless; what must precede policy changes is "task design capable of measuring understanding even in the presence of AI" — without this, only the rules change

[Inst. Report]Source: Generative AI Policies at Top Universities, Oct 2025 · HEPI, Apr 2026

6

OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 — AI Education Inequality as the New Crisis

The OECD's 2026 Digital Education Outlook issues a warning: AI educational opportunities are not being distributed equitably. This could become the structural cause of the next generation's educational inequality.

  • Daily AI tool usage rate: Global North 24.7% vs. Global South 14.1% (Gallup, 2025)
  • Universities with AI guidelines: Europe/North America 70% vs. Latin America 45% (UNESCO, 2025)
  • Untrained faculty: fewer than half of instructors have received AI guidance from their institution
  • Korea/East Asia: among the world's fastest in AI technology adoption, but lagging behind Europe in developing instructional design integration guidelines — the paradox of "fast adoption, slow design" (UNESCO 2025)
  • The "AI fluency gap" is widening faster than the traditional digital divide

🎯 Assessment Redesign 2025
7

The 7 Waves of GenAI Assessment — "Which Wave Is Your Institution On?"

An October 2025 analysis from CUNY mapped two years of university assessment evolution post-GenAI into 7 distinct waves. Institutional maturity diverges sharply from wave to wave.

  • Wave 1: Panic (banning) → Wave 2: Exploration (defining permitted use) → Wave 3: Experimentation (course-level rules)
  • Wave 4: Redesign (restructuring assignment formats) → Wave 5: Integration (internalizing AI as a learning tool)
  • Wave 6: Redefining competencies → Wave 7: Institutionalization (changing curriculum and accreditation standards)
  • Most universities are currently stuck at waves 3–4 — wave 5 and beyond is limited to a small number of leading institutions
  • Hattie perspective: Student self-reported grades (d=1.44) is one of the highest-impact strategies in Visible Learning — the prerequisite for reaching wave 7 (institutionalization) is an assessment structure in which students accurately perceive their own learning level; if AI substitutes this metacognitive awareness, the effect may reverse

[Research]Source: CUNY — Seven Waves of Assessment and GenAI, Oct 2025

8

"AI-Resistant Assignments Are a Myth" — The Shift to Process-Based and Performance-Based Assessment

eSchoolNews (Mar 2026): Assignment designs meant to block AI are doomed to fail. Designing assessments that measure genuine human competency assuming AI exists is now the only viable alternative.

  • "AI-resistant assignment" attempts: surveillance software and detection systems are all unreliable — the more productive question is "How do we measure learning even with AI present?"
  • Performance-based assessment: real-time problem-solving in authentic contexts — directly measuring competencies that emerge without AI assistance
  • Process portfolios: submissions including draft evolution, AI prompt logs, and revision rationale — the process itself becomes the evidence
  • Competency-based shift: from "What must students know?" to "What must students be able to do?"
  • Wiggins & McTighe — Evidence of Transfer: In Understanding by Design (UbD), genuine understanding is only confirmed when it independently transfers to new contexts — AI-generated work contains no transfer evidence, creating a "task achievement ≠ understanding achievement" split; the theoretical basis for performance-based assessment is precisely the direct collection of this transfer evidence
  • Dylan Wiliam — "Assessment IS the activity": Assessment must be a learning experience in itself, not merely a measurement tool — process portfolios are the most powerful implementation of this principle; even portfolios that transparently document AI-assisted work and include student self-reflection become direct implementations of formative assessment

[Media]Source: eSchoolNews — AI-resistant classroom is a myth, Mar 2026 · MDPI — Redesigning Assessments, 2025

EDUCAUSE 2026 — 86% of Students Use AI as Their Primary Research Tool, but Only 10% of Institutions Have Guidelines

Back-to-back EDUCAUSE reports in 2026 covering staff and students reveal a real gap. AI has already spread across higher education, yet a massive divide exists between institutional readiness and the lived reality of members.

  • Staff report (Jan 2026, n=1,960): 70%+ of higher education professionals use AI daily or weekly; 81% are enthusiastic or cautiously enthusiastic — yet more than half simultaneously worry about risks
  • Student report (Mar 2026, n=8,622): 86% of students use AI as their primary research and brainstorming partner — text generation > editing > course material access
  • Institutional gap: only 10% of schools and universities worldwide have AI guidelines (UNESCO, 2025)
  • EDUCAUSE Top 10 2026: "Human Connection" is the central issue amid uncertainty — balancing AI efficiency and educational relationships is the key challenge

[Inst. Report]Source: EDUCAUSE — Impact of AI on Work, Jan 2026 · Students & Technology Report, Mar 2026

10

The CTL's Strategic Role Shift — From "Tool Introducer" to "Instructional Design Partner"

In 2026, the most effective CTLs are not the ones running AI workshops. They are the ones that have evolved into partners who help faculty co-design "in what context, and in what sequence, students should use AI."

  • Columbia CTL Feb 2026: Hosted "Reimagining Teaching and Learning in the Age of AI" forum — institutionalized AI course design sessions for all faculty, expanding community-based practice
  • Washington University "AI Curriculum Corps": CTL-led initiative launched to formally support faculty in integrating AI into the curriculum
  • CU Boulder CTL: Biweekly AI community of practice meetings + 2-day AI Summer Design Studio — a sustained CTL-led learning system
  • Structural challenge: most CTLs are overloaded due to staffing and budget shortfalls — the structural tension between "expanded role expectations" and "resource support" is the biggest barrier (EDUCAUSE 2026)
  • Hattie — Collective Teacher Efficacy (d=1.57): The highest measured effect factor in Visible Learning to date — learning outcomes are maximized when instructors share common goals and strategies and cross-validate their practices; CTL-led communities of practice (CU Boulder) and the AI Curriculum Corps are structures that directly enact this mechanism, qualitatively different from simple AI workshops

[Inst. Report]Source: Columbia CTL 2026 Forum · WashU AI Curriculum Corps · CU Boulder, Feb 2026

🔎 Editor's Lens · This Issue through 5 Pedagogical Theories

Hattie (Visible Learning)

AI adoption without learning science principles falls below the baseline effect size (d=0.40) — it is not which tool is chosen but which cognitive mechanisms are activated that determines effectiveness

Dylan Wiliam (Assessment for Learning)

The integrity crisis is a symptom of assessment design failure — task design capable of measuring understanding even in the presence of AI must come first; feedback that moves learning forward and feedback that merely confirms answers are fundamentally different

Carol Dweck (Growth Mindset)

Repeated AI dependence reinforces the belief that "hard things should be done by AI" — this structurally blocks Productive Struggle, the core experience through which Growth Mindset develops

Wiggins & McTighe (Understanding by Design)

AI can complete an "assignment" but cannot achieve "understanding" — genuine understanding is only confirmed by transfer to new contexts, and AI-generated work contains no transfer evidence

Vygotsky (ZPD · Scaffolding)

Whether AI provides scaffolding within a student's Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), or skips the ZPD entirely to substitute the task — this distinction is the most fundamental question in AI instructional design

※ The perspectives above are editorial interpretations applying each theorist's core framework to the context of AI pedagogy. They may differ from the original context of each theorist's work.
