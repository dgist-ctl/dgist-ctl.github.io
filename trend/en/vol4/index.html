⏱ 30-Second Summary — If You're Busy, Stop Here
The central question of assessment in the AI era is not "Did the student use AI?" but "Does this submission demonstrate the student's learning?"
A 2026 Science policy article reported that AI use and misuse vary sharply by discipline, drawing on a survey of ~95,000 students across 20 U.S. research universities (2023–24 academic year). The implication is not a blanket ban or detection, but discipline- and course-specific assessment redesign.
With its research-, lab-, and project-based courses, DGIST is well positioned to convert drafts, revisions, rationale, verification, and reflection into evidence of learning.
That said, comparative evidence that redesign actually improves learning outcomes is still thin. A one-course pilot, not a full overhaul, is the realistic choice for this term.

🔍 Why Assessment Is Shifting
1

The core problem is not cheating — it is the weakening of evidence

More important than the fact that AI can do the assignment is that a conventional submission may no longer be sufficient evidence of a student's competence.

Generative AI has changed how students write, code, summarize, and review literature. Many assignments now make it hard to confirm "Did the student produce this?" and, further, fail to show "Did the student actually understand this?" The starting point for assessment reform is not tighter surveillance but a redefinition of what counts as evidence of learning.

  • For general reports and summaries, AI drafts quickly. The final product alone reveals little of the student's reading, judgment, and structuring.
  • For simple coding tasks, finished code alone makes it hard to verify problem-solving, debugging, and test-design ability.
  • AI detectors may be a supporting aid, but false positives and evasion make them a poor center of gravity for assessment design.

[Institutional Report]See: EDUCAUSE, The Impact of AI on Learning Assessment (2026) — survey of 438 higher-education faculty and staff (Mar. 2026)

2

Even a large-scale survey points to course-level redesign over a blanket ban

AI use and misuse differ by discipline and task type. So the remedy lies closer to course-level assessment design than to a single university-wide rule.

A 2026 Science Policy Forum article (vol. 392, pp. 818–820), drawing on a survey of ~95,000 students across 20 U.S. research universities, reported that about one-third of students use generative AI regularly for coursework and about 9% used it for cheating. Notably, the disciplines with the highest use are not the ones with the highest misuse. Regular use ran high in computer science (62%) and low in the arts (24%), while misuse ran higher in economics (17%) and journalism (16%) and lower in biology (5%). The finding weighs toward assessment redesign aligned to learning goals and task types rather than the simple prescriptions of "ban AI everywhere" or "solve it with detection tools."

  • Writing, summary, and literature-review tasks are strongly affected by AI drafting. These should also require drafts, feedback uptake, source verification, and oral explanation.
  • For coding and data tasks, test design, error correction, commit history, and code review can be stronger evidence of learning than the final code.
  • Lab and project tasks become less replaceable by AI when they include raw data, lab notebooks, decision logs, and failure analysis.
  • The same survey found AI use varied by gender and racial group as well. An assessment design that does not assume gaps in access and proficiency will widen the fairness problem.
Limits of interpretation — The survey data were collected in the 2023–24 academic year. Tool capability and usage rates have likely shifted since, so read these figures as a floor rather than a current estimate.

[Research]Source: Science 392, 818–820, Generative AI use and misuse call for assessment reform in higher education (2026)

⚖ Redesign Criteria
3

Check an assessment redesign against five criteria

Good assessment is not assessment that bans AI, but assessment in which learning goals and evidence of learning stay aligned even when AI is present.

The openly published 2026 Assessment Redesign Framework is a practical framework for redesigning assignments rather than an efficacy study. This issue condenses it into a checklist for DGIST faculty.

  • Validity: Does it measure actual learning outcomes, not just the final product?
  • Fairness: Does it account for differences in AI access, proficiency, and language ability?
  • Transparency: Are the permitted, restricted, and disclosure rules clear in the assignment brief?
  • Accessibility: Are there diagnostic rubrics, feedback opportunities, and a make-up submission path?
  • Alignment: Are learning goals, activities, and rubric connected?

[Open Resource]Source: Conceived by Hazel Farrell (SETU, GenAI:N3), 2026 Assessment Redesign Framework — CC BY-SA 4.0 (site build by Ken McCarthy) — github.com/kenmccarthy/arf

4

Before detecting AI, run an "assignment stress-test"

Test the assignment before you suspect the student. If AI easily produces a B-grade answer, the assignment is weak by design.

Faculty can paste the existing assignment brief into an AI tool and see how well the AI output meets the submission bar. This is not a cheating investigation but a design diagnostic — a check of whether the assignment truly requires the student's own judgment, verification, and reflection.

  • Step 1: Paste the assignment brief into the AI as-is.
  • Step 2: See how far the AI output satisfies the current rubric.
  • Step 3: Add elements unsolvable without the student's own data, lab records, rationale, or oral explanation.
  • Step 4: Shift the rubric from final-product-centered to process-, verification-, and reflection-centered.

[Open Resource]Source: michael-borck/ai-in-pedagogical-design-and-delivery

🛠 What Faculty Can Do Now

The smallest unit you can change this term:
one assignment, one rubric, one verification step

WhenActionCriterion
Week 1Stress-test one assignment with AICan the AI output reach a B or higher?
Week 2Add one process artifact and one reflection promptIs the student's judgment and revision visible?
Week 3Add an oral check or a short in-class performanceDoes the final product match the student's explanation?
Caution — Do not conclude cheating from a detector score alone. Do not leave AI-use rules vague in the assignment brief. Do not change a whole term's assessment at once. Run process artifacts as sampled checks and credit-for-completion scoring rather than full grading, so the load does not rise for faculty and students alike.
🎓 Course Scenarios
5

Design "AI-resilient assignments" differently by course type

An AI-resilient assignment is not one that blocks AI, but one that leaves a trace of the student's choices, judgment, verification, and failure analysis even when AI is used.
  • Coding / AI courses: final code only → include test design, debugging logs, commit history, code review
  • Lab courses: finished report → include raw data, lab notebook, causes of failure, rationale for design changes
  • Seminar courses: paper summary → include critical questions, cross-paper comparison, limitation analysis, oral defense
  • Project courses: final presentation → include interim artifacts, decision logs, feedback-uptake records, team contribution
  • Convergence foundation courses: right-answer problem sets → include solution path, misconception correction, self-explanation, alternative-solution comparison

[Open Resource]Reference tool (not the direct evidence for the designs above): GitHub Classroom, Use autograding — official documentation

6

PAIRR, Roleplay, and Diagnostic Rubric are not "tools" but ways to collect evidence

The point is not adopting a new tool, but capturing how the student interpreted AI feedback and revised by their own judgment.
  • PAIRR: have students compare peer and AI feedback and reflect on which feedback they adopted.
  • AI Roleplay: cast the AI as reviewer, patient, industry client, or peer researcher to practice Q&A and oral defense.
  • Diagnostic Rubric: diagnose prior knowledge and performance across three levels — Beginning, Developing, Proficient.
  • Repository-based Assessment: use commits, issues, tests, and code reviews as process-centered evidence of learning.

⚠ What Not to Overclaim
7

The GitHub resources are "practical ideas," not efficacy studies

Use this issue's GitHub resources as field-application ideas, not as evidence that a method improves learning outcomes.

Where Vol. 3 dissected a single case grounded in peer-reviewed research and open data, Vol. 4's GitHub resources are different in kind. The Assessment Redesign Framework, Education Toolkit, and AI in Pedagogical Design and Delivery are useful for faculty workshops and assignment design, but are not themselves verified results that "this approach raises learning outcomes."

  • Tier-A evidence: sources such as Science 2026 and EDUCAUSE 2026 that show the assessment crisis is a real higher-education agenda.
  • Tier-B frameworks: sources such as the Assessment Redesign Framework and CO for Education that offer a redesign perspective.
  • Tier-C practical ideas: sources such as Education Toolkit and GitHub Classroom that faculty can adapt.

8

The direction has converged, but evidence verified against learning outcomes is still thin

The assessment-redesign discourse — including this issue's recommendations — is still at the stage of an agreed direction, not a verified prescription.

A 2026 systematic review synthesizing 54 peer-reviewed studies and 6 international policy documents confirms that the field's center of gravity has moved from detection to redesign. Yet the same review notes that those redesign recommendations rest largely on conceptual argument, faculty surveys, and early adopters' experience, and that evidence comparing student learning outcomes over time remains scarce. Agreement on direction and verification of effect are two different things.

  • The redesign forms the literature converges on — oral examinations (vivas), iterative portfolios, in-class writing, collaborative projects, and critical analysis of AI output — match the direction of items 5 and 6 in this issue.
  • What is rare is a comparative design showing that these approaches improve learning. So the evidence is not yet sufficient for full adoption, but sufficient for piloting and observation.
  • On the problem of scale, one proposal is 'viva-lite': a short oral verification appended only to selected tasks, with standardized questions, recording, and interrater checks to shore up validity. Note that this proposal is a journal letter (Educational Researcher 55(5):339–340), not empirical data.
  • This is also an opening for us. Structuring a one-course pilot as a pre–post or comparison-course design would let us answer the question for our own courses, rather than borrowing an answer from large U.S. universities whose class sizes and assessment traditions differ from ours.

[Research]Source: Slimi, Z. (2026). A systematic critical review of generative AI's impact on authorship, pedagogy, and integrity (2023–2025). Frontiers in Education — synthesis of 54 peer-reviewed studies and 6 policy documents (29 Apr. 2026)

🔎 Editor's Lens · What Assessment Reform Asks of DGIST

Paradox — good assessment in the AI era does not put AI at the center

The goal of assessment reform is not to catch AI use but to see learning more clearly. So a good assignment is not "one AI cannot be used on" but "one that cannot be completed unless the student understands, even with AI."

Biggs — a reaffirmation of constructive alignment

AI exposes mismatches among goals, activities, and assessment more quickly. If the goal is critical thinking but the task is submitting a summary, AI amplifies that weakness. Conversely, if the goal is verification, design, and explanation, the rubric must directly require those behaviors.

A DGIST readiness checklist

① pick one high-risk assignment ② run an AI stress-test ③ add one process artifact ④ add an oral check or short in-class performance ⑤ reflect verification, awareness of limits, and reflection in the rubric. A one-course, one-assignment pilot is more realistic than a full overhaul.

Remaining uncertainties

① the accuracy and fairness of AI detectors remain limited. ② process-evidence assessment can increase faculty grading load. ③ differences in AI access remain a fairness concern. ④ which task types transfer best in DGIST's small, highly selective classes must be confirmed by an internal pilot. ⑤ requiring process evidence also places a burden on students — more to submit, and their missteps made visible. Scaling it without checking student acceptance risks producing records that are merely performative.
